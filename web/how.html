How the Encounter lab works

The lab predicts whether a D&D party wins a fight without playing it. Here is where the answer comes from, how accurate it is, and where it breaks.

From dice to a prediction

  1. Simulate

    I wrote a D&D 5e fight simulator: 13 classes, spell slots, Action Surge, Lay on Hands, breath weapons, legendary actions and regeneration. A random party meets random SRD monsters and the fight is played about 100 times to get a reference win chance.

  2. Describe

    Every fight becomes 52 numbers: party hit points, damage per round, how long each side survives, healing available, monster regeneration, and a quick dice-free version of the fight.

  3. Train

    Three gradient-boosted tree models (600 trees each) learn win chance, share of party HP lost and heroes down from those numbers. Training runs on a normal PC in about 15 minutes.

  4. Run in your browser

    The trees are exported as JSON and evaluated by a small JavaScript function. No server is involved. A test checks that the JavaScript and Python give identical predictions.

How accurate is it

Close fights are the weak spot. When the outcome is nearly a coin flip, the dice decide it, so even the simulator disagrees with itself by a few points from run to run.

What it does not know

  • The reference is my simulator, not real tables. The model learns that simulator, flaws included.
  • Missing rules: magic items, control and buff spells, positioning, terrain, resistances and death saves.
  • Simple tactics: every hero and monster follows a fixed, sensible plan, not a clever one.
  • Regeneration is assumed unstoppable. Fire or acid would switch it off for trolls.

Treat the number as a planning aid, not a promise.
